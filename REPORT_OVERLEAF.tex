\begin{abstract}
Rural water security in semi-arid and rainfed agrarian regions depends critically on the optimal placement of on-farm rainwater harvesting reservoirs (farm ponds). Traditional manual surveying across extensive agricultural parcels is labor-intensive, error-prone, and lacks quantitative hydrological runoff modeling. This report presents \textbf{PondPlan AI}, an autonomous geospatial microservice platform and interactive Web GIS application for contour-based farm pond siting, upstream watershed delineation, and expected harvestable water volume estimation. The backend pipeline ingests raw topographic contour elevation survey files in KML/KMZ formats, constructs regularized metric Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) via automated Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) reprojection, and executes hydrological surface runoff modeling using vectorized pure-NumPy algorithms (Priority-Flood depression filling, 2nd-order slope calculation, D8 steepest descent routing, and $O(N)$ topological in-degree flow accumulation). A multi-criteria heuristic identifies the optimal pond outlet site by evaluating upstream accumulation ($70\%$), terrain slope ($\le 8\%$, $30\%$), and concave depression storage depth. Upstream contributing catchments are delineated via reverse-D8 Breadth-First Search (BFS) and converted into simplified WGS84 GeoJSON polygons. An interactive Leaflet.js Web Front-End provides multi-layer satellite and topographic mapping, interactive land area (AOI) bounding-box and polygon drawing tools, real-time precipitation/soil runoff parameter sliders, and real-time visualization of the pond location, catchment boundary, and water harvesting yield. On the reference survey map (\texttt{contours\_1m.kml}), the system isolates an optimal pond outlet at $21.251833^\circ\text{N}, 81.296571^\circ\text{E}$ ($270.0\text{m}$ AMSL) draining a $44,300\text{ m}^2$ ($4.43\text{ ha}$) watershed, yielding an annual harvestable volume of $15,505.0\text{ m}^3$ ($15.51\text{ Million Liters}$) within $2.45\text{ seconds}$ while operating strictly within a 512MB RAM constraint. The service is deployed across the IIT Bhilai campus network at \url{http://10.1.75.51:6241/} (internal port 6000), \url{http://10.1.75.51:5241/} (internal port 5000), and \url{http://10.1.75.51:3241/} (internal port 3000).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Rainwater harvesting through on-farm ponds represents one of the most cost-effective interventions for mitigating agricultural drought, recharging groundwater tables, and sustaining secondary irrigation during dry spells in rural India. Village administrators, agricultural extension officers, and farmers must identify optimal locations for micro-reservoirs that maximize captured surface runoff while minimizing excavation costs and preventing catastrophic embankment erosion.

The goal of this assignment is to develop an automated, web-accessible geospatial platform that accepts topographic elevation contour maps, performs comprehensive hydrological analysis, enables users to select specific land areas directly on a map, recommends optimal farm pond locations, delineates upstream drainage basins, and calculates harvestable water volume. The system is designed as a modular, resource-optimized service capable of running on severely constrained hardware (512MB RAM, 1 vCPU, 1.5GB disk).

\subsection{Motivation}
Manual site selection for farm ponds suffers from several severe limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Topographic Complexity:} Identifying natural drainage channels and calculating cumulative upstream contributing areas across undulating terrain requires analyzing hundreds of thousands of spatial elevation points.
    \item \textbf{Siting Sub-optimality:} Placing ponds too high on ridges captures negligible runoff volume, while placing them in excessively steep valleys risks severe siltation and embankment breach.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Integrated Volume Estimation:} Traditional surveying does not automatically link spatial catchment area with regional rainfall patterns and soil infiltration characteristics to forecast water collection capacity.
    \item \textbf{Field Accessibility \& Resource Constraints:} Remote rural landscapes often lack detailed GIS infrastructure; hence, village planners require an accessible, lightweight web tool that runs without heavy desktop GIS software.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope of the Project}
The system encompasses:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Parsing arbitrary KML and KMZ elevation contour files without hardcoded assumptions.
    \item Constructing continuous metric DEM grids via dynamic local Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) reprojection.
    \item Vectorized hydrological modeling including depression filling, gradient calculation, D8 flow routing, and $O(N)$ DAG flow accumulation.
    \item Multi-criteria farm pond outlet ranking and reverse-D8 upstream watershed delineation.
    \item Interactive Leaflet web front-end featuring map-based land parcel (AOI) selection (bounding box and polygon drawing).
    \item Annual and seasonal harvestable runoff volume estimation using the hydrological Rational Method with customizable precipitation and soil coefficients.
    \item Continuous deployment across mapped network ports at IIT Bhilai.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Out of Scope:} The platform does not execute structural geotechnical embankment engineering, concrete spillway hydraulic structural design, or land ownership cadastral title verification.

    \item \textbf{Response Time \& Latency:} Complete end-to-end contour parsing, DEM generation, hydrological routing, and polygonization executes in under $3.0\text{ seconds}$ for a $3.2\text{km} \times 2.6\text{km}$ survey map.
    \item \textbf{Zero Hardcoding \& Generalizability:} Every coordinate, elevation range, contour interval, and watershed boundary is computed dynamically at request time, ensuring compatibility with any global KML/KMZ dataset.
    \item \textbf{Continuous Availability:} The backend runs with auto-restarting watchdog scripts across internal ports 6000, 5000, and 3000, exposed across the IIT Bhilai campus network.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Terrain and Elevation Analysis}
\paragraph{Automated Metric UTM Projection:} Because geographic coordinates ($\lambda, \phi$) suffer from metric distance distortion that varies with latitude, the dataset's geographic centroid is calculated to identify the appropriate Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) zone:
\begin{equation}
\text{Zone} = \left\lfloor \frac{\bar{\lambda} + 180}{6} \right\rfloor + 1, \quad \text{EPSG} = 32600 + \text{Zone} \quad (\text{or } 32700 + \text{Zone if } \bar{\phi} < 0)
\end{equation}
Contour polylines are densified along segments and projected into metric Cartesian space $(x, y)$. A 2D regular elevation matrix $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ is interpolated at cell resolution $\Delta x = \Delta y = \text{resolution\_m}$ using 2D linear barycentric interpolation followed by nearest-neighbor convex hull perimeter filling.

\paragraph{Priority-Flood Depression Filling:} DEM interpolation inevitably produces artificial topographic sinks. We implement Barnes et al. (2014) Priority-Flood algorithm using a priority queue (\texttt{heapq}) initialized with all boundary cells. Cells are processed inward in $O(N \log N)$ time, raising sink elevations to their lowest pour-point:
\begin{equation}
z_{\text{filled}}(c) = \max\left(z_{\text{raw}}(c), z_{\text{spill}}(n)\right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{2nd-Order Terrain Slope Calculation:} Topographic surface slope is derived via central difference gradient operators:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}_{i,j} \approx \frac{z_{i,j+1} - z_{i,j-1}}{2\Delta x}, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}_{i,j} \approx \frac{z_{i+1,j} - z_{i-1,j}}{2\Delta y}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Slope}_{\text{pct}}(i,j) = 100 \times \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}_{i,j}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}_{i,j}\right)^2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Catchment Area Delineation}
\paragraph{D8 Flow Routing \& $O(N)$ Topological Flow Accumulation:} Runoff is routed to the steepest downward neighbor $j \in \mathcal{N}_8(i)$:
\begin{equation}
S_{i,j} = \frac{z_{\text{filled}}(i) - z_{\text{filled}}(j)}{d_{i,j}}, \quad d_{i,j} = \begin{cases} \Delta x & \text{orthogonal} \\ \sqrt{2}\Delta x & \text{diagonal} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Rather than using recursive flood fills that cause stack overflow on large grids, our engine calculates in-degrees $\text{deg}^-(c)$ (number of upstream tributaries flowing into cell $c$) and resolves the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) in linear $O(N)$ time starting from ridge lines ($\text{deg}^-(c) = 0$).

\paragraph{Multi-Criteria Pond Siting Heuristic:} Candidate cells within the interior margin (and bounded by the user's selected land parcel) are scored via:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}(c) = 0.70 \cdot \frac{\ln(1 + A_c)}{\max \ln(1 + A)} + 0.30 \cdot f(\text{Slope}_c) + \text{DepressionDepth}_c
\end{equation}
where $f(\text{Slope})$ assigns full weight to slopes $\le 8\%$ and penalizes slopes $>8\%$.

\paragraph{Reverse-D8 Watershed Traversal \& Polygonization:} From the selected pond outlet, a Breadth-First Search (BFS) traverses upstream flow vectors. Raster cell bounding boxes are dissolved using Shapely \texttt{unary\_union}, topologically simplified with half-pixel tolerance, and reprojected into WGS84 GeoJSON polygons.

\subsection{Rainfall Data Integration and Water Volume Formulation}
Harvestable water runoff volume is calculated using the hydrological Rational Runoff Method:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{annual}} (m^3) = \text{Area}_{\text{catchment}} (m^2) \times \left(\frac{P_{\text{annual}} (mm)}{1000}\right) \times C
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{Liters}} = V_{\text{annual}} \times 1000, \quad V_{\text{ML}} = \frac{V_{\text{annual}}}{1000}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is annual precipitation (default $1000\text{ mm}$), and $C$ is the runoff coefficient ($0.35$ for agricultural loam, $0.50$ for clay, $0.70$ for rocky terrain, and $0.20$ for sandy soil).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsection{Backend and API Design}
The API is implemented with FastAPI and exposes strict REST endpoints:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \texttt{GET /health} -- Returns service health and uptime status (\texttt{200 OK}).
    \item \texttt{GET /api/sample-kml} -- Streams the preloaded reference \texttt{contours\_1m.kml} for instant front-end demonstration.
    \item \texttt{POST /analyzeContour} -- Multipart form upload accepting \texttt{contour\_map} (and fallback \texttt{file}), \texttt{resolution\_m}, \texttt{min\_catchment\_area\_m2}, \texttt{rainfall\_mm}, \texttt{runoff\_coefficient}, and \texttt{selected\_bounds}. Returns complete structured JSON.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Frontend and Visualization}
The web interface (\texttt{app/static/index.html}) is served directly at the root URL. It features:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Map Overlays:} Leaflet map rendering satellite imagery, shaded catchment boundary polygons, and animated pulsating pond site markers.
    \item \textbf{Land Area (AOI) Drawing Toolbar:} Interactive rectangle and polygon drawing tools enabling users to bound analysis to their specific farm parcel.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Water Volume Calculator:} Real-time sliders for precipitation and soil coefficients with live equivalence metrics (Olympic swimming pools and days of 5-acre irrigation).
    \item \textbf{Export Utilities:} Direct buttons to download the Catchment GeoJSON boundary and export the full JSON report.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Database and Storage}
The service utilizes a stateless, in-memory processing architecture with disk-streamed temporary buffering (64KB chunks) for file uploads. This eliminates disk exhaustion and persistent database bottlenecks, allowing seamless horizontal replication across worker nodes.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
The platform successfully delivers real-time pond siting and catchment delineation. However, two limitations are acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Lumped Infiltration Coefficients:} The Rational Method employs a spatially uniform runoff coefficient $C$. Future versions could ingest spatial soil-type rasters (USDA NRCS Curve Number method) to account for heterogeneous infiltration.
    \item \textbf{Single-Outlet Siting:} The current selector identifies the single highest-scoring reservoir outlet. Extending this to multi-pond cascading systems across hierarchical stream orders would benefit macro-watershed planning.
\end{enumerate}
